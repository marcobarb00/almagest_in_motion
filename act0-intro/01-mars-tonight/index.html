For most of the year Mars drifts slowly eastward among the stars, a little further each night, as the Sun and the Moon do. About every two years and two months it slows down, stops, and runs back westward for two to three months, then stops again and goes on east. Explaining this loop, and predicting where Mars would be, occupied astronomers for two thousand years.

The backward loop of Mars

Figure 1 follows Mars through its next loop after the day you open this page, among the stars it crosses. A day passes in a twelfth of a second, so a season of the night sky goes by in a few seconds.

The loop always comes when Mars is opposite the Sun, rising at sunset and highest at midnight. Each loop falls on average about 50° further east along the zodiac than the last, so it takes 37 loops, 79 years, for one to come back to almost the same stars.

Ptolemy's and Copernicus' explanations of the loop

Ptolemy explained the loop with an epicycle. In his model Mars goes round a small circle, the epicycle, whose center K goes round the Earth on a larger circle, the deferent. When Mars is on the side of the epicycle nearest the Earth, its motion round the small circle is westward and outruns the eastward motion of K, so Mars seems to go back. The radius from K to Mars always points the way the Sun lies from the Earth (Almagest X 6), so Mars is on that near side when it is opposite the Sun, and that is when the loop comes.

Copernicus, in De Revolutionibus (1543), took the Sun as at rest and counted the Earth among the planets, going round the Sun between Venus and Mars (I 10). The loop then comes from the Earth's own motion. The Earth and Mars both go round eastward, the Earth faster, and as the Earth passes Mars on the inside, at opposition, Mars seems to slide back against the stars (V 3).

Figure 2 runs the two models together, with the parameters fitted to modern observations on page 4.2: Ptolemy's with the Earth O at the center, Copernicus' with the Sun ☉ at the center, and Mars P in both.

The two read-outs never differ. Setting Ptolemy's model with the Sun at rest exchanges two sides of a parallelogram: his epicycle becomes the Earth's circle about the Sun, his deferent becomes Mars' circle, and every direction from the Earth stays as it was (page 4.3). No observation of where Mars stands among the stars can tell the two arrangements apart.

In Ptolemy's model the epicycle of each outer planet turns with the Sun, for no reason the model gives. In Copernicus' arrangement it is the Earth's own circle seen from the planet, and its size gives the planet's distance from the Sun, Mars at 1.52 times the Earth's. The predicted directions did not change, and Copernicus' own replacement for the equant made Mars slightly worse (page 4.3).

Which body to take as at rest was argued for a century after Copernicus. Tycho kept the Earth at rest with Copernicus' arrangement of the planets, which gives the same directions again; the phases of Venus, which Galileo reported in 1610, and the parallax of the stars, which Bessel published in 1838, bear on the arrangement (page 4.4).
