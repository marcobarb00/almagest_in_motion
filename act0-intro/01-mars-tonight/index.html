For most of the year Mars drifts slowly eastward among the stars, a little further each night, as the Sun and the Moon do. About every two years and two months it slows down, stops, and runs back westward for two to three months, then stops again and goes on east. Explaining this loop, and predicting where Mars would be, occupied astronomers for two thousand years.

The backward loop of Mars

Figure 1 follows Mars through its next loop after the day you open this page, among the stars it crosses. A day passes in a twelfth of a second, so a season of the night sky goes by in a few seconds.

The loop always comes when Mars is opposite the Sun, rising at sunset and highest at midnight. Each loop falls on average about 50° further east along the zodiac than the last, so it takes 37 loops, 79 years, for one to come back to almost the same stars.
