For most of the year Mars drifts slowly eastward among the stars, a little further each night, as the Sun and the Moon do. About every two years and two months it slows down, stops, and runs back westward for two to three months, then stops again and goes on east. Explaining this loop, and predicting where Mars would be, occupied astronomers from the Greeks to Kepler.

The backward loop of Mars

Figure 1 follows Mars through the loop nearest the day you open this page, the one under way or the next, among the stars it crosses. A day passes in a twelfth of a second, so a season of the night sky goes by in a few seconds.

The loop always comes when Mars is opposite the Sun, rising at sunset and highest at midnight. Each loop falls on average about 49° further east along the zodiac than the last, so it takes 37 loops, 79 years, for one to come back to almost the same stars.

Ptolemy's and Copernicus' explanations of the loop

Ptolemy explained the loop with an epicycle, as Apollonius had before him (De Revolutionibus V 3). In his model Mars goes round a small circle, the epicycle, whose center K goes round the Earth on a larger circle, the deferent. When Mars is on the side of the epicycle nearest the Earth, its motion round the small circle is westward and outruns the eastward motion of K, so Mars seems to go back.

Copernicus, in De Revolutionibus (1543), took the Sun as at rest and counted the Earth among the planets, going round the Sun between Venus and Mars (I 10). The loop then comes from the Earth's own motion. The Earth and Mars both go round eastward, the Earth faster, and as the Earth passes Mars on the inside, at opposition, Mars seems to slide back against the stars (V 3).

To compare the two explanations, Figure 2 runs Ptolemy's model of Mars, with the parameters fitted to modern observations: (a) as Ptolemy set it, with the Earth O at rest, and (b) in Copernicus' arrangement, with the mean Sun ☉ at rest. Mars is P in both.

The outer circle stands for the sphere of the stars, which is so far away that two parallel lines, one from the Earth and one from the Sun, meet it at the same star. In (a) the dial is centered on the Earth, so the line of sight itself points to Mars' place on it. In (b) it is centered on the Sun, and drawn so close that the line of sight from O reaches it elsewhere; the dashed line through ☉, parallel to it, marks the place where the line of sight would meet the distant stars.

Copernicus' arrangement exchanges the roles of Ptolemy's two circles: his epicycle becomes the Earth's circle about the mean Sun, his deferent becomes Mars' circle, and every direction from the Earth stays as it was (page 4.3). This is why the two read-outs always agree. For Mars, directions seen from the Earth decide neither which body is at rest nor which of the two arrangements to choose.

In Ptolemy's model the epicycle of each outer planet turns with the Sun, for no reason the model gives. In Copernicus' arrangement it is the Earth's own circle seen from the planet, and its size gives the planet's distance from the Sun, Mars at 1.52 times the Earth's. The predicted directions did not change, and Copernicus' own replacement for the equant made the largest error in Mars' ecliptic longitude slightly larger (page 4.3).

Which body to take as at rest was argued with physics for a century after Copernicus. Tycho's system, with the Earth at rest and the planets going round the Sun, gives the same directions again. The arrangement can be tested by other observations, though: the phases of Venus, which Galileo announced at the start of 1611, showed that Venus goes round the Sun, which rules out Ptolemy's arrangement of Venus but not Tycho's (page 4.4).

Kepler's eight arcminutes

In 1600 Kepler joined Tycho Brahe in Bohemia, and Tycho set him to work on Mars. Tycho had observed the planets for thirty-eight years with instruments of unprecedented accuracy, and after Tycho's death in 1601 Kepler, his successor as imperial mathematician, went on working from those observations (Voelkel ch. 3; Fitzpatrick § 1.4).

Kepler took the Sun as at rest, with Copernicus, and gave Mars back Ptolemy's equant, on a circle about the Sun. He first placed the equant freely, which served the ecliptic longitudes but not the distances (HAMA pp. 98, 172). With Ptolemy's bisected equant, the circle's positions of Mars differed from Tycho's observations by up to about 8′, a quarter of the Sun's apparent diameter (Fitzpatrick § 1.4).

Figure 3 sets such a circle beside Kepler's ellipse of page 4.5, with the same period and apsides, and its own eccentricity fitted to the ellipse, as Kepler fitted his circle to Tycho's observations. Seen from the Sun, the two directions of Mars still differ by up to 7.5′, close to Kepler's 8′.

Ptolemy's observations could not have shown so small a difference, and with observations a little less accurate, or an orbit a little less eccentric, Kepler might have kept the circle (Fitzpatrick § 1.4). He trusted Tycho's data, and wrote that "these eight minutes alone will have led the way to the reformation of all of astronomy" (Astronomia Nova, transl. Donahue, p. 286).

Kepler looked for another shape of the orbit. Around Easter 1605 he found the ellipse, with the Sun at one focus, and he published it with the law of areas in the Astronomia Nova in 1609 (Voelkel ch. 3). The ellipse is still the first approximation used today for the planets (page 4.5).

We rebuild each of these models from the observations, as its author did, and test it against the modern sky. Every position on this page was measured against the sky that turns each day about the poles, and Ptolemy's first argument in the Almagest is that the heavens move like a sphere (I 3), so the next page starts there.
